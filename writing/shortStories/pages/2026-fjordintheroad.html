Short Story

Click felt he was cruising alone, phone turned off. His kids sat in the backseat, silent, politely occupying a kind part of his mind, perfectly still, invisible, such that their innocence brought him peace yet didn’t impede on his sense of solitude. Un-tense, for a fleeting moment, he even forgot the government chip in his brain. It was a great feeling.

It was also a great feeling to finger past the parking break, shake the 40 ounce can of beer in the cupholder, swig it.

He really should’ve just put his car in self-driving mode, let the computerized horsepower push him along the highway, like a pathetic drunken cowboy. Horseriders could trust their steads to lead the way, whiskey in hand, before Ford put the horses to pasture.

But it was not the 18th century, wild west – the sun had set on that time. The year was 2060 that Click, brother of Clack, cruised in his battered and bruised car. The car was an EV – Electric Vehicle. Make, model: !Gai Nanuq. It’s a weird name for a car, for sure.


!Gai means Heart, feeling, or emotional center in some Khoisan languages. That ain’t an explanation mark, that’s a letter – we don’t got it in English. It don’t sound too different from a woodpecker hitting a tree, finding a grub, and digging in.


Nanuq is an Inuit word. It means polar bear. In their alphabet, it’s spelled like this: ᓇᓄᖅ

It sorta looks like a car’s headlights, don’t it? With a side-view mirror, only on one side.


Click took another sip of his forty. Brand name – Mosquito Hitter. There was a red tinge to the muddy water.

His kids squirmed uncomfortably – two kids, Age 6 and 10. Halved – that’s 3 and 5. Click’s wife, a bruised swooner named Swallow, wanted 4 kids. Click wanted just two. That very night, they reached a happy marital agreement – at 6:00 pm, Click took a single swig of a 5th, agreed to 3 kids as a compromise, they made love, in their bedroom, sparsely adorned with just a few paintings, and a mirror with cracked glass they refused to fix. He left her panting in bed, skin purple in spots, and pulled out the driveway in his battered, crimson car, with the other two kids. And with a forty-ounce beer. For a joyride.

“Quit your bickering back there.” Click said softly through stained teeth, reeking breath. The kids weren’t even speaking at all, they were just fidgeting in their seats, but the squeaks they were making as they moved across the leather cushioning sounded to Click like morse code spelling out “S.O.S.”

The 8 year old, Whish, furtively flicked his eyes to his sister, Lorn. She was staring down with a hard gaze, completely motionless and emotionless.

Lorn rubbed her knee. Whish knew what she meant by that – it was her left knee, the knee that got scarred when Click was drunk and dropped her as a toddler. It was the 3rd worst injury she’d ever received from their father. By rubbing it, Lorn, without speaking, was saying something along the lines of: “We’re at defcon three, this ride. Things could be worse.”

The Nanuq cruised forth while fine pinpoints of light from stars up high provided a slight glow to the road. It had rained the night before – it had melted today. The light reflected off the sad, damp asfault, phosphorescent rays that somehow smelled like sad tears of candlewax drifting off the black slab. It allowed the kids to just barely see their father’s face.

Click was a man with sagging bags under his eyes, purple patches on otherwise white skin, under a buck’s brown irises. He rubbed them a lot – this time of year especially. Allergies.

From his frequent drinking, his skin tended to be a bit bloated and puffy. Out of it sprouted thinning hair and a blonde, peppered beard. His kids had liked playing with his beard as babies, both of them.

Click really resented the fact that his kids wouldn’t let him hold them anymore. That was why he had dropped Lorn that one time – she wriggled and squirmed in the crook of his arm, begging for her autonomy, begging not to be “his little girl” anymore, ready to be “my own person”, and in his stupor, Click clumsily dropped her. It left a permanent red line of scar tissue, an issue on her skin that classmates might make fun of her for – or mock her, rather. “Making fun” is the same as “making merry” – ain’t it?

Lorn was still teasing her scarred skin, scared, with her fingernail. Whish washed her with reassurance from his eyes, silently saying that it’d all be okay.

Click burped.

“Where are we going, pa?” Lorn braved.

“We’re going where we’re going.” he said, waving his hand dismissively, rubbing his left ear. He had an odd way of rubbing his left earlobe, the “Gay Ear”, after he waved folks off like that. His kids didn’t know what a “Gay Ear” was, young as they were, but they were perceptive little tykes. They caught the mannerism. Subconsciously, they had come to associate it with telling someone off.

Click had no piercings, but a tattoo ran down his right arm that Lorn, still rubbing her knee, could see, sitting as she was in the right rear seat. The tattoo ran all the way down from the strap of Click’s wifebeater, down his bicep, down his forearm. It was a long, oppressed trio of tendrils – octopus tentacles. Pink barnacles at the top. The tips of the tendrils ended at his index, middle, and pinkie finger, skipped the ring. He wore a traditional gold wedding band, for his wife.

“Is momma okay?” It had sounded to Lorn like her father was hitting his mother before they left for their joyride. He was.

Click laughed awkwardly, grabbed his Mosquito Hitter, took a swig, and tapped his fingernail against the red drip of blood cumming out of the skin around the mosquito’s piercer. The sound of that metal tap was so, so deafening in that moment. He put the can down, ran his hand along the bleeding patch of skin she’d left under his chin.

“She’s hitting the hay right now, you’ll see her tomorrow. We can garden with her in the mourning. Fertilize the flowers.” His eyes stopped sagging for a moment. “That reminds me, we’re going to get bug spray – you asked me where we were going earlier? Well, there ya go. We need DEET for the garden tomorrow.”

“What’s DEET, pa?”

“It’s what makes you not gotta make the choice between dancing to avoid the mosquitos, impressing them away from your skin, or letting them land and swatting them.”

“...what?”

“Nevermind. It makes the mosquitoes not be able to smell you and land to bite, so you don’t gotta swat them, send them to Nirvana.”

Not knowing much about theology, Whish was still confused. Lorn uttered some all-knowing “hmmm”, though she didn’t really process her pa’s words, either.

Click thought about putting the Nanuq into self-driving mode, letting the computer take the bridles. His finger grazed the glass screen, one menu switched to another, he took his gaze off the waxy highway again and again to navigate the maze of menus, menus, menus.

The nanochip in his brain gave a splintering pain behind his left eye, and the nanoparticles in his blood sent shivers down his spine. A warning. His tires hit a rumble strip, and the car’s suspension did a dance that diddled the passengers back and forth, up and down, quick as a tuning pitch fork vibrates.

“Daddy.” Lorn said simply, in a tone of voice full of so, so much mortal fear and innocent anguish – DEFCON 1. Whish held back vomit.

Click whipped the car back to the straight and narrow, staying in his lane. Both hands on the wheel, not the screen.

“If you want to see your momma's flowerbed tomorrow.” Click intoned, “Without itching and scratching them red welts, then hush up, pups, or I’ll pelt you with pretty little pebbles when we get back to our gravel driveway, and make you stay the night outside.”

Click took another sip, eyes locked on the road. He didn’t want to go off the road again. He had, once. He knew it could happen again. But he somehow couldn’t help himself from the thrill, the escape of a drunken joyride, not even for his kids. Not even with his kids. It sometimes felt like the only way to keep safe from the government chip, brainwashing him. Some damned ethereal Big Brother had installed a quantum computer in his brain, and nanoparticles that carried wave after wave of straining chemicals through his bloodstream, which he utilized alcohol, with great guilt, to mollify.

The chip under Click’s skin monitored his thoughts – not only the thoughts stemming from the neurons in his brain, of course, but also the ones in his gut. Click had a very interesting genome — from birth, Big Brother trusted Click’s gut.

They had miscalculated – there were much better candidates to chip than Click. Click was one of Big Brother’s failed experiments. He couldn’t handle the stress. He sunk to the bottle, and to cartons of stinking cigarettes. Still, agents kept tabs on him, and monitored his chemistry – restricting his free will, his self-agency. They wanted to see how his carbon-based cells responded to silicon-based nanoparticles, sending shockwaves through his system, brainwashing him worse than Pavlov’s dogs.

They wanted to learn what they could from his cells.

They wanted to push him to the brink, until he ended up in a jail cell.

They wanted to learn what they could.

They hoped one day for everyone to be comfortably chipped, to avoid the discomfort it caused Click.

They wanted to learn what they could.

Click rubbed his right temple with his free hand, wishing it all away so he didn’t have to forlornly end each day with a can of beer, or two, three, four. Sometimes much, much more.


Slowly, the family made their way to a gas station, to get some DEET. In about another ten minutes, without too much bickering, they arrived. Click opened the driver door, and the thick, swampy air of Louisiana summer assaulted his face.

The sign of the gas station was blue and red. It said “Fro’s”. Places like here, in the simple year of 2060, old-fashioned diesel was still sold by honest folks. Down in the ‘backwards’ towns of rural Louisiana, far from government dollars. There was an electric charger though, too, by the pumps delivering diesel. Click plugged it into the Nanuq. His children trepidatiously stepped out of the car. Whish heaved again.

“I’ll get you an iced slushie, let your stomach cool off.” part of Click genuinely wished he could buy six-year-old Whish a beer – he feared his son was chipped too, and he needed the release. But he was also afraid for his son to end up a bum like him. So he’d buy him a slushie instead, brand-name: Fjord.

Lorn stumbled, eyes unfocused, completely dissociated. Hearing things she couldn’t articulate to her father in her young age. She was chipped, for sure, Click thought. She’d had seizures as a child that miraculously disappeared after Click agreed to a doctor’s recommendation that he receive a spinal tap, for a disease he didn’t believe he had.

S/he didn’t believe the doctors were Trans-Parent about the nature of the procedure.
